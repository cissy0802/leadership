Is This Boss Worth Following?

Topic: How to Evaluate a Boss·4 principles
"The bird chooses its tree; can the tree choose the bird?" — Zuo Zhuan, Duke Ai, Year 11

Choosing a boss may be the most underrated decision in a career. We spend weeks comparing the pay on two offers, yet often bet the next two years on one person after a conversation or two and a good feeling.

"Worth following" is not the same as "a good person" or "someone I like". The question is: over the next 18–24 months, is it a good bet to tie your career to this person? Answering it takes evidence, not impressions. This issue breaks it into four questions: is their trajectory rising, where did their people go, does their win need your win, and what do they do under pressure.

PRINCIPLE 01 · TRAJECTORY

Is Their Trajectory Rising? Bet on Where They Are Going

direction of scopedistance to the top problem
Principle + source quote
Following a boss is a bet on where they go next. Look at three things: whether their scope is expanding or shrinking, whether their own boss keeps handing them new problems, and how close their charter sits to the organisation's most important problem. When a boss is rising, the extra scope spills over to you; when a boss has stalled, you have to wrestle it away from them.
"If you're offered a seat on a rocket ship, don't ask what seat. Just get on." — Eric Schmidt to Sheryl Sandberg, as told in Sandberg, Lean In, 2013
Scripts
Situation: you have two options. One is a senior manager with a great reputation and a team that has been stable for years. The other arrived recently, and the problem in their hands is the one the company is most worried about this year.
✗ Compare only the people

"The first one is nicer and easier to work with." That may be true, but it answers a different question.

✓ Try: compare trajectories

"A year from now, which side is more likely to have grown? Which is closer to the VP's top problem? Where would I be needed rather than placed?" Whether they are a good person matters a lot; that is the fourth question.

Checklist
  • Has their org grown or shrunk over the past two years?
  • What new problem has their boss handed them recently?
  • Are they in the room when the important decisions get made?
  • How many layers separate their charter from the company's top problem this year?
Common mistakes
  • Mistaking stability for safety: a stable team may be drifting to the edge; nobody has said so yet.
  • Looking only at titles: a big title with shrinking scope is worth less than a smaller title with growing scope.
  • Forgetting that rockets explode: rising businesses carry more risk. Ask about resources and success criteria before you board.
This week's exercise + reflection

Do: sketch how your boss's scope changed over the past two years: what was added, what was taken away.

Reflect: if they were promoted tomorrow, what would happen to you? And if they were sidelined?

PRINCIPLE 02 · ALUMNI

Where Did Their People Go? The Alumni Test

alumni testmultipliersspeaking up for you
Principle + source quote
A promotion record can be dressed up; where someone's people ended up is much harder to fake. Look at their former reports: did they get promoted, did they move on to better roles, and do they still speak well of this boss after leaving? Good bosses let people go to bigger stages, even when it shrinks their own team. Liz Wiseman calls them "Multipliers": under them, people become more capable than they were.
"Peach and plum trees do not speak, yet a path forms beneath them." 「桃李不言，下自成蹊。」 — A proverb quoted by Sima Qian, Records of the Grand Historian, Biography of General Li
Scripts
Situation: you are considering moving under a director. In the conversation, they talked at length about their philosophy of growing people.
✗ Take their word for it

"They care a lot about growth; it should be fine." Everyone cares about growth in an interview.

✓ Try: ask the alumni

Find two of their former reports, one still there and one who has left, and ask a single question: "What did you get out of those years working with them?" Ask about their experience; don't reveal your plans. What the one who left says is often the more useful answer.

Checklist
  • How many of their former directs were promoted in the past three years?
  • Has anyone moved from their team to a bigger role, with their help?
  • Do the people who left still speak up for them?
  • Do they speak up for people, or only give advice?
Common mistakes + women's angle
  • Asking only those who stayed: the people still around them are already a filtered sample.
  • Confusing "good to me" with "grows people": the two often diverge.
  • Women's angle: Ibarra, Carter and Silva (2010, Harvard Business Review) analysed a Catalyst survey of more than 4,000 high-potential employees and found that women received no less mentoring, but their mentors were typically more junior, and men were more likely to be promoted as a result of being mentored. So tell apart a boss who advises you from one who speaks up for you in the meetings that matter.
This week's exercise + reflection

Do: list five people who reported to your boss over the past five years, and write down where they are now.

Reflect: five years from now, whose alumni list do you want to be on?

PRINCIPLE 03 · INCENTIVES

Does Their Win Need Your Win? Alignment Beats Goodwill

incentive alignmentstructure vs character
Principle + source quote
Even good people put what they are measured on first when it counts. So don't only ask "is this a good person?"; ask "does my success count toward theirs?" If your project sits near the top of what they are measured on, they will fight for resources on your behalf. If your project competes for people with their top commitment, friction will come sooner or later, and it will be friction caused by structure, not by character.
"It is not from the benevolence of the butcher, the brewer, or the baker, that we expect our dinner, but from their regard to their own interest." — Adam Smith, The Wealth of Nations, Book I, Chapter II, 1776
Scripts
Situation: you see a new direction worth investing in early, but your boss keeps insisting that you protect their own commitments first. Neither of you gives way.
✗ Blame the person

"They can't see the bigger picture; they lack vision." That conclusion makes it hard to keep working together, and it may not be right.

✓ Try: look at the incentives first

"They are accountable for delivering that line of work, and my new direction isn't in what they are measured on. Blocking me is them doing what they are measured on." Then go to someone accountable for both sides and get the priority settled. Afterwards, watch how quickly they turn: if they turn fast once the call is made, the problem was incentives, not vision.

Checklist
  • Where does my work rank among what they are measured on this year?
  • If I succeed, do their results look better?
  • Who else is competing with me for their attention?
  • What is their own boss measuring them on?
Common mistakes
  • Reading misaligned incentives as character: put someone else in the same seat and they would probably do the same.
  • Thinking a better boss will fix it: if the incentives don't line up, the same conflict comes back wherever you go.
  • Betting before aligning: even when you are right about the direction, get the person who evaluates you on board before you move resources.
This week's exercise + reflection

Do: write down the top three things your boss is measured on this year, and check whether your work is among them.

Reflect: if your project failed, what would they lose?

PRINCIPLE 04 · UNDER PRESSURE

Watch Them When the News Is Bad Character Shows in Hard Winters

character is destinysmall promisescan't vs won't
Principle + source quote
In good times everyone looks like a good boss; the real person shows under pressure: when a project slips, do they shield people or push them forward; do they credit the team or take it themselves; do they keep small promises; how quickly do they turn around when proven wrong? Also tell "can't" apart from "won't": some bosses are content to do one thing well and don't want a bigger stage. They can be excellent bosses, just not necessarily the vehicle for your next chapter.
"Only when the year grows cold do we see that the pine and cypress are the last to fade." 「岁寒，然后知松柏之后凋也。」 — The Analects, Book IX (Zi Han)
Scripts
Situation: you want to know what a prospective boss is like under pressure, but you have never worked for them.
✗ Listen to their stories

"They say the team matters most to them." How someone behaves under pressure never appears in their self-introduction.

✓ Try: ask about one specific time

"The last time a project went badly wrong, how did you report it upward? What happened to the person who was responsible?" Then check the same event with their reports. Two versions that match are more credible than any philosophy.

Checklist
  • When things break, do they say "we" or "they" to their own boss?
  • Who got the credit in the report upward?
  • Did they deliver the small thing they promised last time?
  • How long did it take them to turn around after being proven wrong?
Common mistakes
  • Falling for the honeymoon: when you are new, or arrive bringing resources, of course they are warm. You see their real trade-offs at the first conflict of interest.
  • Reading "won't" as "can't": a boss who doesn't want a bigger stage can still be a very good boss. If you leave, leave well; the relationship will matter in review meetings for years.
  • Judging right after a rupture: what you conclude about someone right after a fight is often a justification for a decision you have already made. Wait before you decide.
This week's exercise + reflection

Do: recall the hardest moment of pressure your current boss has faced: what did they do?

Reflect: is the way you behave under pressure the way your own reports would want to follow?

Crossovers

  • Psychology · Kahneman's structured interview (1955): at 21, Kahneman redesigned the Israeli army's interviews: rate six traits one by one first, and only at the end close your eyes and give an intuitive overall score. It predicted far better than the old free-form interview, and intuition that came after the structure was useful too. Alike: judging a person starts by breaking the question apart. Unlike: when choosing a boss, you are evaluating and being evaluated at once.
  • Evolutionary biology · Image scoring in cleaner fish (Bshary & Grutter, 2006): cleaner wrasse pick parasites off larger fish but sometimes cheat by biting off mucus. Waiting fish watch how a cleaner treats the client before them and prefer the one that just behaved honestly. Unlike: a fish watches once; you can look at five years of alumni.
  • Distributed systems · Chaos Monkey (Netflix, 2011): Netflix deliberately kills servers at random in production, because a system's real resilience only shows during failure. Unlike: you can't inject failures into a boss; you can only find the ones they have already been through.
  • Buddhism · the Kalama Sutta: the Buddha told the Kalamas not to believe something because of hearsay, tradition, or "this is our teacher", but to verify it for themselves. Alike: don't judge a person by reputation. Unlike: the Buddha was talking about practice; you are verifying a career bet.

Ten takeaways

  1. Choosing a boss is a bet on an 18–24 month trajectory; "worth following" is not the same as "a good person" or "someone I like".
  2. Trajectory: when a boss's scope is expanding, opportunities spill over to you; when it is shrinking, you have to wrestle them away.
  3. The closer a boss is to the organisation's top problem, the more the same effort gets amplified.
  4. Alumni: a promotion record can be dressed up, but where a boss's former reports ended up is hard to fake.
  5. When checking on a boss, the people who left them are often more informative than the ones who stayed.
  6. Tell apart a boss who gives you advice from one who speaks up for you in the meetings that decide promotions.
  7. Incentives: the question is not whether they are a good person, but whether your success counts toward theirs.
  8. Friction from misaligned incentives is a structural problem; someone else in the same seat would probably act the same way.
  9. Under pressure: whether a boss shields people or pushes them forward when things break says more than anything said in good times.
  10. A boss who doesn't want a bigger stage can be a good boss, just not necessarily the vehicle for your next chapter; when you leave, keep the relationship.

Going deeper

What should I read to go deeper?
  • Liz Wiseman, Multipliers (2010): the difference between leaders who multiply and leaders who diminish, usable as a checklist against a real boss.
  • Herminia Ibarra, Nancy Carter and Christine Silva, "Why Men Still Get More Promotions Than Women" (Harvard Business Review, 2010): the difference between a mentor and a sponsor.
  • Sylvia Ann Hewlett, Forget a Mentor, Find a Sponsor (2013): how to find someone willing to speak up for you. Day 44 on this site also covers sponsorship.
  • Jeffrey Pfeffer, Power (2010): why it pays to be close to the parts of an organisation that handle its core problems.
  • Daniel Kahneman, Thinking, Fast and Slow (2011), chapter 21: why structured judgment beats intuition.
Following has a time limit
It works best to follow a boss for a purpose you can name: learning a capability, taking on a kind of scope, or getting through a promotion. Review the four questions every six months. When the purpose is met, or conditions change, re-evaluate. Leaving a good boss is not betrayal; staying with the wrong one is a failure to look after yourself.
When does this way of seeing mislead you?
The biggest bias is information asymmetry: you have collected years of your current boss's flaws and none of the new one's, so the familiar person always loses the comparison. Measure both with the same ruler. Another bias is mistaking "agrees with me" for "has vision": what matters is whether their judgment is sound on questions where neither of you has a stake.
How will AI change this question?
If agents take over more and more execution work, a boss's value in handing out tasks shrinks, and their value in winning you scope and speaking up for you in reviews grows. At that point the alumni test will matter more than how capable the boss is personally.
