Decision Frameworks for Everyday Work

Topic: Decision Frameworks at Work·4 principles
"There is surely nothing quite so useless as doing with great efficiency what should not be done at all." — Peter F. Drucker, 1963

Many of the hard problems in management are the same few questions coming back again and again: Is this worth doing, and how well? How fast should we decide? Who should do it, and who gets the final say? How do we talk when we disagree? Each question has a ready-made framework you can borrow, so you don't have to think it through from scratch every time.

The four principles in this issue map to those four questions. A framework earns its keep by forcing you to ask the question you would otherwise skip; the answer still takes judgment.

One rule of use runs through all of them: use the framework to organise your own thinking, and state the conclusion in language the other person can accept. Put a matrix straight in front of someone and it can land as a lecture, or as an "objective" tool you're using to overrule their judgment.

PRINCIPLE 01 · HOW GOOD IS GOOD ENOUGH

Be Excellent at a Few Things Choose where to excel — and where good enough is the goal

reward–cost matrixopportunity costdeliberate good enough
Principle + source quote
Ask two questions about everything on your plate: how much does doing it excellently pay off, and how much does getting it wrong cost? High-payoff work deserves investment. Work with limited payoff but a high cost of failure should be just good enough — and rock solid. Where both are low, do the minimum or cut it. Seniority isn't doing everything perfectly; it's knowing which one or two things deserve it.
"There is surely nothing quite so useless as doing with great efficiency what should not be done at all." — Peter F. Drucker, "Managing for Business Effectiveness," HBR, 1963

The companion question: every time you say yes, what gets pushed out? That's opportunity cost.
Scripts
Situation: a new quarter starts, and the team's maintenance list holds a quarterly access audit, a redesign of the internal wiki, a performance fix promised to a customer, and a weekly auto-generated report nobody has opened in months.
✗ Both reactions waste the team

"These all matter, let's do every one well." — Every one ends up done to about 70%.
"Audits? Just get through it." — Nothing happens for a while, then one failure becomes a big deal.

✓ Try: sort into boxes first, then assign people

"The performance fix is the one we excel at this quarter, so it gets people first. For the audit, the goal is just good enough but solid: one owner, every check that can be automated is automated, and a reminder two weeks before it's due. The report pauses for a month — let's see if anyone asks. The wiki waits this quarter. I'll share this table; raise any disagreement this week."

Checklist
  • What specific payoff does doing this excellently bring? Can I name it?
  • If this goes wrong, what's the worst outcome? Can it be undone?
  • For the "just good enough" work, is there an owner, automation and early warning?
  • The thing I just said yes to — what did it push out?
Common mistakes + a note on the women's angle
  • Treating "good enough" as "ignore it": the goal in the bottom-right box is no incidents, not no investment. Work you handle reactively tends to fail at the worst possible moment.
  • Not saying "good enough" out loud: you are deliberately investing less, but others may see a dropped ball. Write the tradeoff down and have the people affected confirm it.
  • The women's angle: Babcock, Recalde, Vesterlund and Weingart (2017, AER) found that tasks with low promotability are more often asked of women, and women more often say yes. When you sort your own work into the matrix, watch whether the bottom-left box keeps growing.
This week's exercise + reflection

Do: list everything you're working on and place each item in one of the four boxes. Count how many truly need to be excellent.

Reflect: the last time you made a "just good enough" task excellent, whose time did it cost?

PRINCIPLE 02 · HOW FAST TO DECIDE

Two-Way Doors Fast, One-Way Doors Slow Reversibility sets the speed

one-way / two-way doorspremortemwords you can't take back
Principle + source quote
First ask: if this decision is wrong, can we walk it back? If yes, it's a two-way door — decide quickly and correct as you go. If not, it's a one-way door — slow down, get more eyes on it, and work out how it could fail before you commit. Most decisions are two-way doors, yet organisations often run them through one-way-door processes, and everything slows down.
"But most decisions aren't like that – they are changeable, reversible – they're two-way doors." — Jeff Bezos, 2015 Letter to Shareholders
Scripts
Situation: the team has spent two weeks arguing over which charting library to use for an internal dashboard. The same week, the field names for an external API were settled in a ten-minute meeting and sent to three partners the next day.
✗ The doors are swapped

The charting library can be swapped any time, yet it's being debated like a one-way door. The external API is hard to change once anyone integrates with it, yet it was decided like a two-way door.

✓ Try: label the door before deciding

"The charting library is a two-way door: we pick one, and if it doesn't work in two weeks we switch — no more meetings. The API fields are a one-way door: let's run a premortem first. Imagine that a year from now this design is causing us real pain — what most likely went wrong? Then two people who've built interfaces like this review it, and we decide next week."

Checklist
  • If this decision is wrong, what does walking it back cost?
  • Am I holding a third meeting in front of a two-way door?
  • For one-way doors, have I run a premortem?
  • After deciding, did I set a date to come back and check?
Common mistakes
  • Treating every decision as a one-way door: when everything needs alignment and review, the organisation gets too slow and misses the window.
  • Treating two-way doors as never needing a second look: reversible doesn't mean no review. A two-way door only works if you actually come back.
  • Forgetting the words you say: commitments, judgments and comments about other people blurted out in a meeting are often one-way doors — once said, they can't be unsaid. The more off-the-cuff the question, the more it pays to check which door you're at.
This week's exercise + reflection

Do: for the next two weeks, mark each decision you make with "one-way" or "two-way". See which kind of door your time actually goes to.

Reflect: is the decision your team has dragged out longest really a one-way door?

PRINCIPLE 03 · WHO DECIDES

Put Each Decision at the Right Level Leverage, four levels of delegation, one owner

leveragefour levels of delegationDRI
Principle + source quote
A manager's output is the output of the organisation they lead. So the key question isn't "how do I do this well?" but "which work, done by me, has the most leverage?" Sort the team's recurring decisions into four levels: I decide; I decide after hearing you; you decide and tell me; you decide. Every decision has exactly one directly responsible individual (DRI).
"A manager's output = The output of his organization + The output of the neighboring organizations under his influence." — Andrew S. Grove, High Output Management, 1983
Scripts
Situation: a newly promoted manager has to approve almost every technical choice the team makes. They review dozens of code changes a day, and the team is constantly waiting on them.
✗ Every "let me take a look" adds to the queue

Each time you say it, one more thing waits on you. You become the team's most expensive bottleneck, and the team never learns to judge for itself.

✓ Try: assign levels to recurring decisions

"I've listed the ten kinds of decisions we make most often and given each a level: interface changes — you decide and tell me; new external dependencies — I decide after hearing you; internal implementation and code style — you decide, and I stop reviewing. Each kind gets a DRI. In a month we'll see which ones can move down a level."

Checklist
  • Of what I did today, what could someone on the team have done without the result getting worse?
  • Has every kind of recurring decision been given a level?
  • Does every piece of work have exactly one DRI? Is anything "owned by two people"?
  • When someone made a decision I disagreed with, did I overturn it?
Common mistakes
  • Handing over the task but not the decision: saying "you own it" while demanding updates and approvals at every step means you handed over nothing.
  • One reversal undoes it all: overturn a decision you delegated even once, and much of the trust you built goes with it. When you disagree, ask for their reasoning first.
  • Joint ownership: when two people both "own" something, often nobody does. When two people co-lead, every item still needs to land on one name.
This week's exercise + reflection

Do: go through this week's calendar and mark every meeting and task "only I can do this" or "someone else could". Pick one from the second group and hand it over next week.

Reflect: if you took next month off, which decisions would stall?

PRINCIPLE 04 · WHEN YOU DISAGREE

Interests Before Positions Ask why before arguing about what

positions vs interestsstate your needsdisagree and commit
Principle + source quote
A position is "what I want"; an interest is "why I want it". Two people's positions are often opposed while their interests can both be met. So the first step in any dispute is translating both positions into interests. The same goes for speaking up yourself: state your needs and reasons rather than offering a single solution. Once the decision is made, commit to it fully, even if you disagreed.
"Your position is something you have decided upon. Your interests are what caused you to so decide." — Roger Fisher & William Ury, Getting to Yes, 1981
Scripts
Situation: two weeks before a release, an engineer insists on rewriting an old module first, and the PM insists the date can't slip. In the meeting each repeats their own case, and neither persuades the other.
✗ Competing on who holds out longer

"It has to be rewritten" versus "no delays, period." Either one side loses and resents it, or the decision drags on even longer.

✓ Try: ask each of them why

The engineer wants to stop being woken up every week by this module's failures; the PM wants to keep a commitment to a major customer. "Then we ship on time, put the two most failure-prone endpoints behind a flag, and rewrite them in the first iteration after release. I'll cover on-call for these two weeks."

Checklist
  • Can I say in one sentence why the other person wants what they want?
  • Am I stating a need, or a solution that allows only one answer?
  • Is there an option that meets both sides' interests?
  • After the decision, am I holding back on executing it?
Common mistakes + a note on the women's angle
  • Presenting a solution as a need: "I want X" gives the other side one option. "What I need is…, so I lean toward X, and I'm open to a better way" leaves them room.
  • Agreeing out loud, executing half-heartedly: disagree clearly before the decision; executing half-heartedly afterwards damages trust more than open disagreement.
  • The women's angle: Bowles, Babcock and Lai (2007) found experimentally that women who negotiated for higher pay were penalised in evaluations more than men who did the same; Bowles and Babcock (2013) then found that explaining the request in terms of team and organisational relationships reduced that cost. Leading with interests is a negotiation technique here, and also a layer of protection.
This week's exercise + reflection

Do: the next time you disagree with someone, hold back your solution and ask first: "What worries you most?"

Reflect: behind the last position you held firm on, what was the real interest?

Crossovers

  • Psychology · Simon's satisficing (1956): Herbert Simon argued that rationality is bounded, and most real decisions stop at the first option that is good enough — he called it satisficing. "Deliberate good enough" is satisficing on purpose. The difference: Simon described what happens under constraint, while here you actively choose which things not to optimise.
  • Investing · Taleb's optionality and the barbell (Antifragile, 2012): Taleb argues for favouring bets with limited downside and large upside, arranged as a barbell — one end extremely safe, the other small high-payoff bets, nothing in the middle. The reward–cost matrix is the same barbell: keep high-failure-cost work solid, bet boldly where the payoff is high and failure isn't fatal, and cut what's low on both. The difference: Taleb is describing the shape of payoffs; the matrix is about allocating limited attention.
  • Distributed systems · sagas and compensating transactions (Garcia-Molina and Salem, 1987): instead of holding a global lock, a long transaction is split into small steps that can each be undone by a compensating action, buying speed. A two-way door is the same idea: make sure you can undo, and you don't have to be slow. The difference: in a system, undo can be designed in advance; words once spoken rarely have a compensating action.
  • Distributed systems · Raft's single leader (Ongaro and Ousterhout, 2014): Raft allows only one leader to accept writes per term, so two nodes never write conflicting data in a "split brain". A DRI works the same way: if two people can both make the call, the organisation splits its brain. The difference: when a Raft leader fails, a new one is elected automatically; when an owner goes missing in an organisation, often nobody notices.
  • Buddhism · attachment to views: among the five wrong views listed in the Abhidharma is dṛṣṭi-parāmarśa — clinging to one's own views as the best. Holding a position no matter what is exactly that clinging. The difference: Buddhism asks you to let go of attachment to views themselves; negotiation only asks you to see the needs beneath a position, and the needs themselves can stay.

Ten takeaways

  1. Four questions keep recurring in management — is it worth doing, how fast to decide, who decides, how to handle disagreement — and each has frameworks to borrow.
  2. Use a framework to organise your own judgment, then state the conclusion in language the other person can accept; don't use it to overrule people.
  3. The reward–cost matrix asks two things: how much doing something excellently pays off, and how much getting it wrong costs.
  4. Work with limited payoff but a high cost of failure should be just good enough and solid: an owner, automation and early warning.
  5. A deliberate "good enough" must be said out loud and written down, or others may see a dropped ball.
  6. Reversible decisions are two-way doors to take fast and fix as you go; irreversible ones are one-way doors that need a slower pace and a premortem.
  7. Much organisational slowness comes from running two-way doors through one-way-door processes, while spoken words are often one-way doors.
  8. In Grove's terms a manager's output is the team's output, so first ask which work, done by you, has the most leverage.
  9. Delegate recurring decisions across four levels and give every decision exactly one directly responsible individual (DRI), so nothing is owned by two people.
  10. A position is what someone wants and an interest is why they want it; translating positions into interests often reveals an option both sides can accept.

Going deeper

Where do these frameworks come from?
One-way and two-way doors come from Jeff Bezos's 2015 letter to shareholders; the premortem from psychologist Gary Klein (HBR, 2007); the output equation and leverage from Andy Grove's High Output Management (1983); positions versus interests from Fisher and Ury's Getting to Yes (1981); the term DRI is usually credited to Apple's internal culture. The reward–cost matrix has no single source — it's a close relative of the risk matrix (likelihood × impact) and the Eisenhower matrix (important × urgent).
When do frameworks mislead you?
Three cases: you bend the facts to fit the boxes; the classification itself becomes the argument ("is this really a one-way door?"), which means you're short on information, not frameworks; and you use the framework to win a discussion rather than to think something through. A framework only forces the right question; the answer still takes judgment.
How do I turn them into habits?
A one-month plan. Week one: run everything on your plate through the reward–cost matrix. Week two: label each decision as a one-way or two-way door. Week three: assign levels and DRIs to the team's ten most common decisions. Week four: at every disagreement, first ask "what worries you most?" Practising one at a time sticks far better than using all four at once.
How will AI change these judgments?
Prototypes, analyses and first drafts are all getting cheaper, so many former one-way doors become two-way doors and the range of things you can do fast grows. But the real one-way doors — external commitments, people decisions, the things you say — don't shrink; they stand out more. And as execution gets cheaper, the judgment of which work deserves excellence becomes more valuable.
