Leading at a Bigger Scope: Scale Yourself, Lead Senior People, Lead Managers

Topic: Leading at a Bigger Scope·4 principles
"Governing many is like governing few: it is a matter of division." — Sun Tzu, The Art of War, "Momentum"

When your leadership is considering giving you more scope, they tend to ask three kinds of questions: how will you scale yourself, how will you lead senior engineers at your own level, and how will you lead managers? Behind all three is the same turn: from getting things done yourself to getting things done through others.

The first three principles each answer one of those questions. The fourth covers what runs underneath all three: why people want to do good work for you. "Going deeper" at the end covers how to answer these questions when you are asked them.

PRINCIPLE 01 · SCALE YOURSELF

From Doing the Work to Building a System That Runs Itself Scale Yourself by Building a System

DRIwritten capacityleading signals
Principle + source quote
At a bigger scope you can't be present at every step. Do three things: split the outcome into a small number of workstreams, each with exactly one DRI and one measure; set a steady operating cadence, and write down which calls the DRIs make, which you make, and which go up; keep your own time for cross-org alignment, talent, and the two or three biggest risks. Write down cross-team contributions: how many people, what share of their time, which quarter, which milestones, and who signed off. A verbal promise falls through at the first priority conflict.
"A manager's output = The output of his organization + The output of the neighboring organizations under his influence." — Andrew S. Grove, High Output Management, 1983
Scripts
Situation: your boss asks, "If the charter grows, how will you know early that a workstream is in trouble, without being in the details?"
✗ State a principle

"I'll set up a review process and look at the metrics." Everyone says that.

✓ Try: name the leading signals

"Every week each DRI gives a confidence level on their next milestone, and I watch the trend: if it drops from high to medium, I ask, without waiting for red. A cross-team dependency blocked for more than a week escalates automatically. A status that jumps straight from green to red I treat as a reporting problem. Most important, when someone raises a risk early, I thank them in front of the team."

Checklist
  • Does every workstream have exactly one DRI?
  • Are cross-team contributions written down, and who signed off?
  • Is it clear which calls belong to the DRIs, which to me, and which go up?
  • Now that the scope is bigger, what am I going to stop doing?
Common mistakes
  • New scope, old habits: if you still review every design, you become the bottleneck for the whole organisation.
  • Watching only the status colour: the useful signal is in the trend. By the time it turns red, it's late.
  • Two DRIs on one line: when two people own it, nobody does.
This week's exercise + reflection

Do: list the three things you spent the most time on this week, and mark the one a DRI should be doing.

Reflect: if you took two weeks off, which workstream would stall first?

PRINCIPLE 02 · LEAD SENIOR PEOPLE

Authority Comes From Trust, Not Title Lead Senior Engineers Through Trust, Not Title

real ownershipdecision rightshandling disagreement
Principle + source quote
When you lead senior engineers at your own level, you can't out-know every one of them. Your authority comes from three things: give them the hardest problems, define the outcome rather than the steps, scope the work so success counts toward their next level, and speak up for them in the rooms they're not in; split decision rights, with technical calls in their area theirs, and priorities, trade-offs and cross-team commitments yours; and treat them as peers, with feedback going both ways. When you disagree, restate their view until they agree you've got it. If the call is easy to reverse, let them try their way for one cycle with an agreed checkpoint; if it's hard to reverse, lay it all out, and if needed go to your boss together. Once it's decided, the team hears one voice.
"Use the phrase 'disagree and commit.' This phrase will save a lot of time." — Jeff Bezos, 2016 Letter to Shareholders
Scripts
Situation: a senior engineer disagrees with a priority call you made and, instead of coming to you, goes straight to your boss.
✗ Treat it as going over your head

Confront them, or rush to prove to your boss that you were right.

✓ Try: treat it as a signal that you weren't aligned

Thank your boss for telling you and ask them to send the engineer back to you. Sit down and restate their view until they nod, then give yours. Afterwards, set a norm: come to me first; if we can't agree, we go to our boss together. Escalating is fine. Going around each other is the problem.

Checklist
  • Are their problems hard enough for their level?
  • Which technical calls have I explicitly handed to them?
  • When did I last ask them for feedback on me?
  • When we disagreed, did I restate their view first?
Common mistakes + women's angle
  • The stronger your technical skills, the bigger the risk: grabbing the call in someone else's area. Ask first, then offer.
  • Pulling rank: with peers, that card gets weaker every time you play it.
  • "I told you so": say it once and nobody tells you the truth again.
  • Women's angle: Catalyst's 2007 report "The Double-Bind Dilemma for Women in Leadership" found that women leaders are often caught between being seen as competent but not liked, or liked but not competent. Agreeing decision rights up front and leading with questions lets your authority rest on the rules rather than on your tone, and lowers the risk of being read as "too aggressive".
This week's exercise + reflection

Do: sit down with one senior engineer and write down how decision rights are split between you.

Reflect: the last time you made a call in someone else's area, was it because you were more right, or just faster?

PRINCIPLE 03 · LEAD MANAGERS

Lead the Team Through Them, Not Around Them Lead Through Your Managers, Not Around Them

first 30 daysskip-levels for signalgrowing managers
Principle + source quote
Leading a manager comes in three steps. For the first 30 days, listen and align: learn their goals, how their team is doing and what is already working, and agree on outcomes, decision rights and how to escalate, changing nothing in the meantime. Then manage outcomes and standards, not their team: hold them accountable for results, team health and how they treat people; use skip-levels to gather signal, not to give direction; speak with one voice in public and disagree in private. Finally, grow them into a better manager. If you came up as a TLM, watch for one risk in particular: diving into the technical details of their team.
"When the work is done and the task accomplished, the people all say, 'We did it ourselves.'" 「功成事遂，百姓皆谓：我自然。」 — Tao Te Ching, Chapter 17
Scripts
Situation: you have just started leading a manager. In a skip-level, an engineer on their team complains about a technical approach.
✗ Weigh in on the spot

"Yes, that approach is a problem. I'll talk to them." You have just knocked down their standing with their own team.

✓ Try: hand the signal back to them

"Thanks for telling me. Have you talked to them about it?" Later, tell the manager privately what you heard and ask what they think. They handle it; you help only if they need you.

Checklist
  • In the first 30 days, am I listening, or already changing things?
  • Besides delivery, do I hold them accountable for team health and how they treat people?
  • Does what I hear in skip-levels make its way back to them?
  • Do I know how they want to grow?
Common mistakes
  • Going around them to direct their people: faster in the short term; in the long term the team has two bosses and doesn't know whom to follow.
  • Evaluating without developing: the biggest difference between leading managers and leading engineers is that you teach them to hire, run performance conversations and handle conflict.
  • Rushing to change things after taking over: fix things before you understand what works, and you often break what was fine.
This week's exercise + reflection

Do: for a manager you lead now or will lead, write a one-page list of questions for the "listen first" 30 days.

Reflect: if this manager left tomorrow, what would their team say they learned from them?

PRINCIPLE 04 · MAKE PEOPLE WANT TO

Why People Do Good Work: Autonomy, Competence, Relatedness Autonomy, Competence, Relatedness

hygiene factorsself-determination theorythe progress principle
Principle + source quote
The first three cards rest on one question: why do people want to do good work for you? First, guard the floor, what Herzberg called "hygiene factors": fairness, a sustainable workload, psychological safety. Without them people are dissatisfied; with them, they are not automatically satisfied. Then meet the three basic needs of self-determination theory (Deci and Ryan): autonomy, a say in how they work; competence, doing well and growing; relatedness, being cared about and having people to fight alongside. Finally, make progress visible.
"Of all the things that can boost emotions, motivation, and perceptions during a workday, the single most important is making progress in meaningful work." — Teresa Amabile and Steven Kramer, "The Power of Small Wins", Harvard Business Review, 2011
Scripts
Situation: an engineer who has always been engaged has clearly lost energy. Their performance is still fine, but they no longer volunteer ideas.
✗ Performance is fine, so leave it

Or pile on more work to "keep them busy".

✓ Try: look for the cause in the three needs

In the 1:1, ask about the person before the work: "What's been going well lately, and where do you feel stuck?" Then check the three needs: have they lost their say, is the challenge too easy or too hard, or do they feel isolated? Clear one blocker first, so they can see progress.

Checklist
  • Do they have a say in how they work?
  • Is their challenge a little beyond what they can do today?
  • Do I know what matters to them?
  • When a decision went against them, did I explain why?
Common mistakes
  • Trying to buy morale: hygiene factors remove dissatisfaction; they don't buy engagement.
  • Rewarding results but not recognising progress: on long projects, the people who can't see progress lose heart first.
  • Reversing someone's work without explaining: few setbacks hurt more.
This week's exercise + reflection

Do: score your own current situation on the three needs and find the lowest one.

Reflect: on your team, whose need has been threatened lately, and which one?

Crossovers

  • Cybernetics · The law of requisite variety (Ashby, 1956): a regulator must have at least as much variety as the system it regulates, or it can't keep it under control. One person's attention can't keep up with the variety of a large organisation, so judgment has to be handed to the DRI of each workstream. Unlike: a regulator doesn't need to be trusted.
  • Distributed systems · The φ accrual failure detector (Hayashibara et al., 2004): instead of declaring a node simply alive or dead, it computes a continuous level of suspicion from heartbeat intervals and acts only past a threshold, much like a weekly confidence trend. Unlike: people may fake their heartbeat out of fear, which is why psychological safety matters.
  • Neuroscience · Predictive coding (Rao & Ballard, 1999): higher levels of the brain send predictions down, and lower levels send only "prediction errors" up. Leading managers works the same way: you set expectations, they report surprises, and what you hear in skip-levels is the error signal. Unlike: the brain doesn't have to protect the middle layer's standing.
  • Biology · Stigmergy (Grassé, 1959): termites build without a commander; each one decides its next move from the traces the last one left in the environment. Shared dashboards and written-down capacity are an organisation's traces. Unlike: termites have no priority conflicts.

Ten takeaways

  1. At a bigger scope, your output is your team's output plus that of the neighbouring teams you influence, so you scale by building a system, not by doing the work.
  2. Every workstream has exactly one DRI, and cross-team contributions are written down with headcount, share of time, time frame and who signed off.
  3. Catch risk early with leading signals: falling confidence, a dependency blocked for over a week, a status that jumps from green to red.
  4. Making it safe to raise bad news early matters more than any dashboard.
  5. When leading senior engineers at your level, authority comes from trust and clear decision rights, not title.
  6. Technical calls belong to senior engineers; priorities, trade-offs and cross-team commitments belong to you; in their area, ask before you offer.
  7. In a disagreement, restate the other person's view first; let them try what's easy to reverse, and lay out or escalate together what isn't.
  8. Lead managers through them, not around them: listen for the first 30 days, and use skip-levels only to gather signal.
  9. The biggest difference between leading managers and leading engineers is that you have to grow them into better managers.
  10. People do good work when their needs for autonomy, competence and relatedness are met, and they can see progress on meaningful work.

Going deeper

What should I read to go deeper?
  • Andrew Grove, High Output Management (1983): a manager's output and leverage.
  • Camille Fournier, The Manager's Path (2017): the later chapters cover managing managers.
  • David Marquet, Turn the Ship Around! (2012): replacing orders with people saying "I intend to".
  • Tanya Reilly, The Staff Engineer's Path (2022): understanding how the senior engineers you lead think.
  • Liz Wiseman, Multipliers (2010): leaders who make people smarter, and leaders who make them smaller.
  • Teresa Amabile and Steven Kramer, The Progress Principle (2011): why small wins light up a whole day.
  • Amy Edmondson, The Fearless Organization (2018): why psychological safety brings bad news early.
All seven are covered by, or on the list for, Deep Reading on this site.
How do I answer "hypothetically, if you were given more scope"?
This is usually an assessment of capability, not a negotiation. Give three points and one real example per question, in under two minutes. Talk about how you would do it, not how the organisation should be designed, and don't ask "so are you giving it to me?" Naming one of your own risks, such as "I'm strong technically, so I tend to step in", comes across as more mature. You can close by asking, "From your view, what would success look like in the first six months?" If you're asked what you need, talk about needs, not solutions.
What if I end up reporting to someone new?
It's fine to be honest about the human side, for example that after years alongside your current team there's some sadness, and then turn to the positive: build trust early, and align on what you own, what you can decide, your team's goals, how progress is tracked and how you raise problems early. If you have needs, that is the moment to state them, once.
How will AI change these three turns?
As agents take over more execution work, scaling yourself will depend more on how you design DRIs and decision rights, and leading people will shift from handing out tasks to judgment, trade-offs and creating opportunities for others. The fourth principle won't change: people still need autonomy, competence and relatedness.
